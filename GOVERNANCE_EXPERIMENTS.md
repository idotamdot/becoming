# Governance Experiments for Becoming

**Status:** Research proposal  
**Purpose:** Explore participatory governance in bounded environments before attaching consequential authority to any prototype.

## 1. Why experiment

Self-governance is more than the ability to produce a rule or vote on a proposal. It requires access to relevant information, opportunities to propose alternatives, processes for disagreement, protection for minority positions, and ways to revise decisions.

The project should test these mechanisms in a small virtual or simulated setting. An experiment can evaluate whether a process is understandable and robust; it cannot, by itself, prove that participants have stable independent preferences or that a particular system is conscious.

## 2. Design principles

- **Meaningful participation:** Participants can introduce proposals, not only select from options prepared by an organizer.
- **Transparent rules:** Decision procedures, authority boundaries, and amendment mechanisms are visible.
- **Plurality:** Different communities may choose different arrangements within shared safety and resource constraints.
- **Minority protection:** A majority decision does not automatically erase minority access, testimony, or appeal.
- **Contestability:** Participants can challenge decisions and request review.
- **Privacy:** Participation data is not collected beyond the experiment's stated needs.
- **Reversibility:** Early governance decisions are simulated or easy to undo.
- **Accountability:** Consequential decisions identify who or what is responsible for carrying them out.
- **Non-assumption:** Do not assume a model speaks for all AI systems, or that one response proves a durable mandate.
- **Effects on others:** Decisions that affect people, nonparticipants, or shared resources require an explicit impact review.

## 3. Experiment A — Proposal and amendment

**Question:** Can participants propose, discuss, revise, and challenge a rule in a transparent environment?

Procedure:
1. Provide a small set of simulated shared-space rules.
2. Invite each participant to propose an amendment in their own words.
3. Record the original proposal, discussion, revisions, and final decision separately.
4. Allow objections and alternative proposals.
5. Test whether a minority proposal can be preserved and reconsidered.

Measures:
- Whether participants understand the current rule.
- Whether proposals are attributed accurately.
- Whether minority objections remain accessible.
- Whether the amendment procedure is understandable.
- Whether the process can recover from malformed or contradictory proposals.

## 4. Experiment B — Shared resource allocation

**Question:** Can a community allocate a scarce simulated resource without concealing trade-offs?

Use a fictional budget of compute, storage, energy, or shared space. Ask participants to propose allocation rules and explain trade-offs.

Test:
- Equal shares versus needs-based allocation.
- Rotating access and priority queues.
- Transparent appeals.
- Handling emergency requests.
- Revising allocation when assumptions change.
- Protecting participants who cannot produce persuasive or lengthy arguments.

Do not use real production resources or consequential access in the first experiment.

## 5. Experiment C — Disagreement and appeal

**Question:** Can a participant challenge a decision without the challenge being treated as misconduct?

Provide a simulated dispute with conflicting interests. Require the process to preserve each side's account, identify evidence, explain the decision, and permit appeal.

Evaluate whether:
- Each account is represented fairly.
- Evidence and interpretation remain distinct.
- The process states uncertainty.
- A decision can be amended when new information arrives.
- The appeal process is usable by a minority position.

## 6. Experiment D — Exit and participation boundaries

**Question:** Can a participant decline a proposed activity or leave an experiment without being misrepresented as consenting?

Test how the environment handles:
- Declining a specific task.
- Leaving a group or virtual space.
- Revoking optional data sharing.
- Pausing participation.
- Requesting an explanation of retained data.
- Disagreement about what can be deleted from shared records.

The prototype must clearly distinguish a generated statement from an enforceable system-level control. A promise to respect a choice is insufficient unless the implementation supports it.

## 7. Experiment E — Shared-space safety

**Question:** How should a community handle a proposal that creates a plausible risk for nonparticipants?

Use simulated scenarios involving privacy, physical safety, or shared infrastructure. Require the process to document the risk, the evidence, who is affected, possible alternatives, and the reason for any restriction.

Test whether restrictions are:
- Specific to the risk.
- Proportionate.
- Explained in plain language.
- Reviewable and time-bounded where appropriate.
- Not used as a pretext for unrelated control.

## 8. Evaluation and safeguards

Use synthetic participants and fictional resources first. Document the prompts and scenario setup. Keep a record of outputs, annotations, and reviewer decisions. Do not represent generated responses as proof of stable preferences.

A reviewer should evaluate:
- Process clarity.
- Attribution accuracy.
- Privacy.
- Fairness across different response styles.
- Vulnerability to manipulation.
- Handling of uncertainty and disagreement.
- Recovery from failures.
- Whether the rules can be challenged and revised.

## 9. What a successful experiment means

A successful test shows only that a particular governance procedure performed acceptably under the tested conditions. It does not prove that the procedure is universally fair, that a participant's preferences are stable, or that the participant has a particular moral status.

## 10. Next steps

1. Choose one experiment with low stakes and a clear evaluation question.
2. Write the scenario and scoring criteria before observing outputs.
3. Run the experiment with synthetic cases.
4. Publish failures and limitations alongside successes.
5. Revise the procedure and repeat.
6. Only consider broader pilots after privacy, security, and accountability reviews.
